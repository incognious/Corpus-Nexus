Mysteries & Anomalies

Tartaria / Old World

A modern hidden-history theory built around old maps, grand architecture, world fairs, mud flood claims, and the idea that a previous civilization was erased or misrepresented.

Evidence: Level 06 - Modern Lore Status: Historical term plus modern theory Reliability: Mixed; cartographic term documented, empire claims disputed Shelf: Mysteries & Anomalies

Historical Tartary

Tartary, or Tartaria, was a real term used by European writers and mapmakers for large parts of northern and central Asia. It appeared on maps for centuries, often covering regions associated with Siberia, Central Asia, Mongolia, Manchuria, and other lands that Europeans poorly understood.

This does not automatically mean there was a single hidden worldwide Tartarian empire. Historically, "Tartary" often functioned as a broad outside label rather than the name of one unified state.

The Modern Old World Theory

Modern Tartaria theory uses the old map term as a doorway into a much larger claim: that an advanced old-world civilization once built grand cities, monumental architecture, and possibly advanced energy systems, and that this civilization was later erased, renamed, or folded into official history.

The theory often connects Tartaria to mud flood claims, orphan trains, world fairs, reset events, old photographs, unexplained architecture, buried lower floors, and missing or rewritten history.

Old World Building Gallery

These buildings are not proof of Tartaria by themselves. They are visual case studies: grand civic, rail, exhibition, opera, and government buildings often discussed in Old World research because of their scale, ornament, construction timelines, or symbolic presence in major cities.

Core Claims

Documented

Tartary Appears on Historical Maps

Old maps really do use names such as Tartary, Great Tartary, Chinese Tartary, Independent Tartary, and Little Tartary. The key question is what those labels meant to the mapmakers.

Documented

Grand Old Architecture Exists Worldwide

Large civic buildings, train stations, courthouses, exposition halls, cathedrals, and government buildings were built in ornate classical, Beaux-Arts, Gothic, and neoclassical styles across many countries.

Interpretive

Architecture as Evidence of a Lost Civilization

Old World researchers argue that the scale and craftsmanship of certain buildings do not fit the official timelines, technology, labor accounts, or population levels of the cities where they appear.

Speculative

Mud Flood and Buried Floors

Mud flood theory claims that many cities were partially buried by a catastrophic event, leaving lower windows, basement doors, and sunken levels as clues. Conventional explanations usually include grading, street raising, flooding, redevelopment, and basement construction.

Speculative

World Fairs as Temporary Cover Stories

Some claim that world fair buildings were not temporary constructions, but inherited old-world structures later demolished to hide evidence. This claim needs case-by-case research because many exposition buildings were intentionally built quickly with temporary materials.

Speculative

Free Energy and Atmospheric Technology

Some theories claim domes, towers, spires, and metal ornamentation were part of a lost energy system. This is one of the most speculative parts of Tartaria research and needs careful separation from documented architectural symbolism and engineering.

Highly Speculative

Global Reset

The strongest version claims a worldwide reset erased a prior civilization, repopulated cities, rewrote history, and replaced original inhabitants with new populations. This is a major claim and requires major evidence.

Research Method

For each claim, start local. Pick one building, one map, one city, or one event. Find original maps, construction permits, newspaper records, architect names, photographs, city grading records, flood records, demolition notices, and population data. The archive should prefer specific cases over huge claims.

Use four labels: documented fact, plausible question, speculative theory, and unsupported leap. Tartaria research becomes stronger when it stops treating every old building as proof and starts building a case file around exact places and dates.

Open Questions

Is Tartaria best understood as a misunderstood map term, a symbol for forgotten architectural skill, a clue to hidden history, or a modern myth built from real anomalies and unresolved questions?
