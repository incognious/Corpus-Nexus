Questioning the Globe

A visual look at Earth curvature, distance, perspective, and long-range observations.

Curvature Per Mile

The common 8 inches per mile squared formula.

One common simplified calculation says the curve drop is approximately 8 inches times the distance in miles squared. This is a tangent-based shortcut used in many debates, but it does not include observer height, atmospheric refraction, elevation, camera zoom, or terrain.

Example Calculation

1 mile is about 8 inches of drop. 2 miles is about 32 inches. 3 miles is about 72 inches. 10 miles is about 666 inches, or about 55.5 feet.

Long-Distance Visibility

If Earth curves, why can some distant objects still be seen?

People often debate ships visible at long distance, city skylines across water, mountains seen from far away, and lake or ocean horizon observations.

Claimed Problem

Objects are visible too far away. Critics argue that a globe model predicts more hidden height than the observation appears to show.

Mainstream Explanation

Elevation, refraction, camera zoom, lens effects, atmospheric conditions, and exact observer height can affect visibility and apparent horizon behavior.

Water Level Question

Does Water Curve?

Large bodies of water appear flat to the human eye, which raises a visual and philosophical question: why does water always seek level, and how should level be defined on a spherical Earth?

Questions Raised

What would curvature look like across lakes, oceans, and canals? How large must the distance be before curvature is visible? What measurements would separate visual flatness from geometric flatness?

Mainstream Explanation

On a globe, level means perpendicular to gravity at each local point. Under this explanation, the surface can be curved globally while still being locally level to an observer.

Perspective vs. Curvature

Two explanations for bottom-up disappearance.

One debate asks whether distant objects vanish from the bottom up because of perspective alone, curvature alone, or a combination of optics, geometry, and atmosphere.

Perspective Model

Parallel lines visually converge toward the horizon. The question is whether apparent convergence can hide lower portions of objects.

Globe Curvature Model

A curved surface blocks the lower portion of distant objects after accounting for observer and target height.

Questions to Consider

What would count as a strong test?

  • How much curvature should be visible over 5, 10, or 20 miles?
  • How much does observer height change the result?
  • How much does atmospheric refraction affect visibility?
  • Can camera zoom restore objects that are hidden by curvature?
  • What observations would clearly prove or disprove the model?
  • What details must be recorded: distance, elevation, lens, weather, pressure, humidity, and exact target height?
